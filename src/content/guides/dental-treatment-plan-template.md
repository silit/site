For many patients, the treatment plan is the document that decides whether they book. It is often the first time they see their own mouth described in writing, with prices next to it. A plan that is clear, complete and easy to read builds trust before the patient ever sits in the chair. A plan that is a list of codes and numbers does the opposite.

This guide covers what a dental treatment plan template should include, how to lay it out, and the mistakes we see most often in clinics that treat international patients.

## What a dental treatment plan should include

| Section | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Patient name, date and plan number | Lets you match the plan to records and to later versions |
| Diagnosis in plain words | Patients accept treatment they understand, not treatment they are told about |
| Tooth chart with treatment per tooth | The fastest way to show what will happen where |
| Treatment list with the fee for each item | Shows what the total is made of |
| Phases and visits | Explains how many trips or appointments it takes, and the healing time in between |
| Alternatives, including no treatment | Part of informed consent, and it shows you are not pushing one option |
| Materials | Zirconium crowns, emax crowns or emax laminate veneers are not the same product, and patients compare them |
| Total, deposit and payment terms | Removes the most common reason for a slow reply |
| Validity date | Prices and plans change; a date avoids disputes months later |
| Clinician name and signature | Puts a person behind the plan |

### Use one tooth numbering system everywhere

Most clinics use the FDI two-digit system (11 to 48). Whatever you use, use the same numbering on the chart, in the treatment list, in the consent forms and in the patient passport. A patient who sees "UR6" on one page and "16" on another will ask, and a coordinator will have to answer.

### Explain phases and healing time

Implant treatment is the classic example. Placement and the final crowns are usually separate visits, with a healing period in between that your clinician sets for each case. If the plan does not say this, patients plan one trip and are surprised by the second. Write each phase as its own block: what happens, how long it takes, and when the next visit is.

### Show what the price includes

"Full arch implants: €X" invites questions. "Four implants, a temporary bridge on the day, the final zirconium bridge at the second visit, X-rays and check-ups included" answers them before they are asked. If hotel or transfers are part of a package, list them in the plan or link to the package.

## How to present the plan

**Keep it to one page.** Details can sit in an appendix or in the consent forms. The plan itself should be readable in a minute.

**Lead with the chart.** A smile map with colour for each treatment type tells the story faster than a table. Put the legend right next to it, with a count for each treatment.

**Write for the patient, not the lab.** "Root canal treatment on the lower left molar" is better than "RCT 36". Keep the clinical code in brackets if your team needs it.

**Walk through it.** Send the plan as a PDF, then go through it on a short call or video. Most questions are about the same three things: how many visits, what the price includes, and what happens if something goes wrong.

**Follow up.** A plan without a follow-up message within a day or two is often a plan that goes cold.

## Common mistakes

- Clinical shorthand with no plain-language version
- No alternatives listed, so the patient compares with another clinic's plan instead
- Prices without a validity date
- Different tooth numbering in different documents
- Materials named by brand on one page and by type on another
- No signature or clinician name, so the plan feels like a quote from a call centre
