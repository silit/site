Rank trackers answer one question well: where does my page sit in Google for this keyword? AI answers do not have positions in the same way. ChatGPT, Gemini or Google's AI Overviews write a short answer, name a few businesses and cite a handful of sources, and the next answer to the same question may look different.

An AI visibility tracker exists to measure that. This guide covers what it should measure, how to read the results and what to check before you choose one.

## What to measure

### Share of answers that name you

This is the headline number. Ask a set of questions, collect the answers, and count how many mention you. If 10 answers about "bookkeeping app for freelancers" name you in 4, your visibility for that keyword is 40 percent.

It works because it accepts that answers vary. Instead of chasing one perfect answer, you measure how often you are part of the conversation.

### Mentions with a link

Being named is good. Being named with a link to your site is better, because that is the version that sends visitors. Track the two separately: some engines mention brands without linking, others link generously.

### Who is named instead

Every answer that skips you names someone else. Over a few weeks, the list of rivals shows who the engines consider the default choice for each question. It is often a different list from your Google competitors.

### The sources behind the answer

When an engine searches the web, it reads pages before it answers: review sites, comparison articles, directories, forums, sometimes your own pages. The sources that appear again and again are where your effort pays off, because they shape many answers at once.

### Search demand

A keyword that nobody searches is not worth much visibility. Put monthly search volume next to each keyword, so you fix the ones with real demand first.

## How many answers you need

One question asked once is anecdote. For each keyword, a useful sample is:

- two or more customer questions, such as one that asks for a recommendation and one that asks about price or fit;
- every engine you care about, because each one searches and cites differently;
- the same check repeated each week, so a trend can form.

On six engines, two questions per keyword on the four chat engines plus the search itself on Google AI Overviews and AI Mode gives 10 answers per keyword per check. That is enough to see a pattern without drowning in data.

## How to read the numbers

- **Watch the trend, not the week.** A drop from 50 to 40 percent in one week can be noise. Three weeks in a row is a signal.
- **Compare like with like.** Results change with location and language. Keep both fixed for a keyword, and track a second market as a separate keyword if you sell there.
- **Leave out questions that contain your brand.** "Is Tallyhawk good for invoicing?" will name you almost every time and inflates the score.
- **Separate engines before you average them.** A 30 percent average can hide 80 percent on one engine and zero on another. The zero is where the work is.
- **Read the actual answers now and then.** Numbers tell you where to look. The text tells you why an engine prefers a rival: a price point, a feature, a review site it trusts.

## What a tracker should do for you

Before you choose a tool, ask:

1. **Which engines does it check, and with live web search?** Answers from a model without search say little about what customers see today.
2. **Does it ask real customer questions, or only the keyword?** People ask AI in sentences.
3. **Can you see the raw answers, rivals and sources?** A score without the evidence behind it is hard to act on.
4. **Does it tell you what to change?** Tracking alone shows the problem. The value is in knowing which page to fix and how.
5. **How is it priced?** Most tools charge by the number of questions or keywords and how often they are checked. Work out the cost for the keywords you actually need.
